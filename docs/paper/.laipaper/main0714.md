Laboratory automation accelerates synthetic biology, but robotic programming remains a barrier that excludes most researchers. Existing large language model (LLM) automation tools, designed for chemistry with defined small molecules and static protocols, fail when applied to synthetic biology because they overlook four unique challenges: sequence-level biosecurity, the context-dependent behaviour of living systems, integration with community standards, and support for distributed collaboration across laboratories. Here we present LabscriptAI, an execution-aware agent framework that couples two control loops — an authoring loop and a runtime loop — to translate natural language into validated robot scripts and support runtime self-recovery. On a 90-task benchmark, LabscriptAI achieved a 96.7% simulation-pass rate,  outperforming LLM-based and commercial baselines. We validate LabscriptAI against each of these four challenges: built-in biosecurity screening before wet-lab execution, cell-free characterization of 298 GFP variants from 53 student teams with automated data deposition to FPbase; a 7-fold improvement of a formaldehyde-utilizing enzyme in whole-cell biotransformation with physical containment; and quality-assured preparation of 531 parts into 500 Distribution Kits shipped to iGEM teams worldwide. By addressing the unique demands of synthetic biology, not just liquid handling, LabscriptAI turns any benchtop into a secure, community-ready biofoundry.

Laboratory automation is transforming synthetic biology by enabling high‑throughput, reproducible experimentation1-4. Affordable liquid‑handling robots such as Opentrons have democratized hardware access5, and community projects like AssemblyTron have simplified DNA assembly automation6. Yet scripting remains a stubborn bottleneck7,8: translating a biological protocol into robot‑ready code demands programming expertise that most biologists lack. This barrier, compounded by platform heterogeneity across Opentrons, Tecan, Hamilton, and other systems, excludes the majority of researchers from automation and concentrates it in well‑resourced centers.
Recent efforts have explored large language models (LLMs) to orchestrate robotic experiments from natural language. Coscientist11 and ChemCrow12 demonstrated LLM‑driven chemical synthesis automation, in settings with defined small‑molecule reagents, predictable stoichiometry, and minimal biosecurity constraints. When applied to synthetic biology, they encounter four fundamental mismatches. First, chemistry automation manages toxic or flammable reagents, whereas synthetic biology requires sequence‑level dual‑use screening (e.g., DNA synthesis for toxins), a concept absent from chemical platforms. Second, chemical reactions follow reproducible kinetics, but biological systems (cells, cell‑free extracts) are noisy, context‑dependent, and require iterative adaptation, not static recipes. Third, synthetic biology operates through shared resources (iGEM, Addgene, FPbase, SBOL); automation must integrate with version‑controlled repositories and quality‑assured distribution, a scale rarely seen in chemistry. Fourth, hundreds of teams worldwide share genetic parts and protocols; automation should support this distributed collaboration, not isolate it. Existing LLM tools treat biology as “wet chemistry” and fail on all four counts.


Here we introduce LabscriptAI (Figure 1), an execution‑aware agent framework that translates natural-language experimental intent into validated, executable scripts for liquid‑handling robots. Beyond lowering the general programming barrier of laboratory automation through targeted, interpretable code refactoring, LabscriptAI is designed around four requirements specific to synthetic biology. It enforces responsible research through biosequence screening, physical containment directives, and mandatory human‑in‑the‑loop oversight. It supports living systems by executing and recovering cell-free and whole-cell workflows on context-dependent, and at times hazardous, biological substrates. It builds on community standards by turning shared, version-controlled protocols into validated execution packages and depositing results back into community repositories (protocols.io, FPbase, and others). And it enables distributed collaboration through community-scale preparation and quality-assured global sharing of parts and protocols. We validate LabscriptAI across four real‑world scenarios that directly correspond to these requirements—biosecurity, living systems, community standards, and distributed collaboration—showing that native synthetic biology automation is not only possible but already operational at community scale.

System architecture
LabscriptAI implements an execution-aware agent harness for synthetic-biology automation (Figure 1). Rather than partitioning intent specification, protocol authoring, and robot execution into role-specialized stages connected by handoffs, the harness integrates them within a single stateful control loop (Supplementary Note 1). System state, validation evidence, action authority, and provenance therefore remain available throughout the workflow, rather than being reconstructed or transferred at each stage boundary. This continuity is particularly important for biological assays, in which instrument-state feedback may need to interrupt, redirect, or trigger recovery of an ongoing run. Planning, execution, sensing, and recovery are coordinated within this same loop. Spanning the full workflow (Figure 1a)—from human-confirmed intent through package authoring, simulator-checked execution packages and gated physical execution to memory capture and reuse—the design accommodates context-dependent living-system behavior while aligning with community standards and responsible-research norms.

The LabscriptAI harness operates through two coupled control loops—an authoring loop and a runtime loop (Figure 1b)—each driven by a specialized agent that interleaves reasoning and action to execute code and make targeted file edits⁴⁶. This design accounts for the recurrent nature of synthetic biology automation, in which protocol refinement, simulator errors, runtime anomalies and recovery outcomes can each feed back into earlier decisions rather than following a single pass. In the authoring loop, simulator feedback guides code revision; at runtime, instrument-state feedback guides gated recovery. Four shared resources provide the persistence layer carrying the harness invariants across both loops: a protocol and hardware library, biosecurity and containment directives, execution traces and manifests, and case memory of recovery actions and outcomes.


Benchmark performance
To evaluate LabscriptAI’s ability to generate synthetic biology automation scripts, we developed a 90‑task benchmark (Supplementary Dataset 1) adapted and expanded from Inagaki et al.9. Tasks were stratified as Easy (n=15), Medium (n=39), Hard (n=29) and Expert (n=7). Baselines comprised direct LLMs (GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8 and Gemini 3.5 Flash), the coding agents Codex (GPT-5.5) and Claude Code (Claude Opus 4.8), the commercial system OpentronsAI and a published GPT‑4 fix‑loop9 (Table 1). Agent systems provide system-level comparisons; architectural contributions were assessed through DeepSeek-v4-flash ablations.
LabscriptAI achieved the highest simulation-pass rate (SimPass) among the evaluated systems, passing simulation for 87 of 90 tasks (96.7%; Table 1). Direct LLMs passed 43–74 tasks; Codex, Claude Code, OpentronsAI and the published GPT‑4 fix‑loop passed 83, 73, 69 and 59 tasks, respectively. Across difficulty levels, LabscriptAI passed simulation for 14/15 Easy, 38/39 Medium, 28/29 Hard and 7/7 Expert tasks.
Ablation studies (Table 1) quantified the contribution of individual architectural components. Replacing the Precise Refactoring Engine with full‑script regeneration (w/ Rewrite repair) lowered SimPass from 96.7% to 93.3% (84/90), indicating a modest advantage for targeted SEARCH/REPLACE corrections by this metric. Removing domain‑specific knowledge retrieval lowered SimPass to 92.2% (83/90), and disabling the iterative repair loop lowered it to 85.6% (77/90). Removing all agent loops and tool‑mediated repair (Base LLM only) lowered SimPass to 47.8% (43/90), indicating that the authoring loop and feedback‑driven repair contributed to performance beyond the base model alone.
Error‑pattern analysis (Extended Data Table 1) revealed distinct failure modes. Direct LLM implementations exhibited hardware‑constraint violations, API syntax errors, and references to unsupported hardware capabilities. OpentronsAI showed improved API compliance but encountered logical errors in complex pipetting sequences. LabscriptAI failures involved edge cases in deck configuration and multi‑dispense logic.
During benchmark evaluation, LLMs occasionally refused to generate scripts for certain workflows, citing biosafety and biosecurity concerns (Table 1). These refusals underscore the importance of balancing risk mitigation with legitimate use. LabscriptAI addresses this through a multi‑layered safety framework (described below and in Figure 1) that includes explicit human oversight, biosequence screening, physical containment for hazardous workflows, and procedural safety instructions, enabling responsible code generation without sacrificing access to high‑risk, high‑value experiments.
Cross‑platform validation of community plate reader calibration protocol 
To test LabscriptAI’s ability to generate cross‑platform, hardware‑agnostic scripts, we selected the iGEM Plate Reader Fluorescence Calibration protocol16 as a proof‑of‑concept challenge. This community standard, hosted on protocols.io, enables conversion of arbitrary fluorescence units to standardized fluorescein equivalents, allowing reproducible comparison across laboratories, a quintessential example of synthetic biology’s reliance on shared resources. A collaboration agent (Extended Data Fig. 4) was implemented to interface with the protocols.io API, retrieving the latest protocol version and later depositing the generated scripts. The natural language input to LabscriptAI was simply: “Prepare a fluorescein standard curve following the iGEM protocol” (Figure 2a). LabscriptAI translated this request into a structured procedure: preparation of 10 μM fluorescein stock, serial 2‑fold dilutions across seven concentrations, and triplicate plating in 96‑well black plates with 200 μL final volumes (Figure 2b).
LabscriptAI then generated platform‑specific scripts for four liquid‑handling systems (Figure 2c): Opentrons OT‑2 and Flex (native Python API17, Supplementary Fig. 1), Hamilton Vantage (PyLabRobot10), and Tecan Fluent. For Tecan compatibility, we developed pyFluent, a standardized Python API (Extended Data Fig. 5). The Precise Refactoring Engine automatically produced targeted modifications for hardware‑specific constraints – pipette tip compatibility, deck layout differences, and liquid‑handling protocols, without any manual intervention. Experimental validation yielded coefficients of variation below 5 % for triplicates and linear regression R² values exceeding 0.999 across all systems (Figure 2c). These results demonstrate that LabscriptAI can translate a single natural language protocol into executable, platform‑optimized scripts, turning a community‑defined standard into a turnkey automation workflow.
Robotic screening of GFP variants for student competition 
To validate LabscriptAI in a biological discovery context, we first automated a cell‑free protein synthesis (CFPS) workflow for GFP expression on an Opentrons Flex (Figure 3a). The natural language input “Set up cell‑free GFP expression reactions with varying template concentrations”, coupled with the commercial kit protocol, generated scripts for master mix preparation, template dilution series, and 37 °C incubation (Figure 3b, Supplementary Fig. 2). The parent avGFP from Aequorea victoria18 (Supplementary Table 1) showed robust expression: CFPS reactions in 25 μL volume yielded strong fluorescence across template concentrations from 0.25 to 7.50 ng μl⁻¹, with 5.0 ng μl⁻¹ producing 0.130 ± 0.001 μM fluorescein equivalents after 3 hours (Figure 3c).
We then deployed LabscriptAI in the Critical Assessment of Protein Engineering (CAPE) competition19, a community challenge modeled after CASP for protein structure prediction20. Student teams develop computational models, submit designs for centralized characterization, and receive standardized performance data (Figure 3d). For the 2025 CAPE challenge, engineering GFP variants with enhanced fluorescence, teams received 147,950 sequence‑function data points from previous studies22,23 for model training, then submitted six designs each containing up to six mutations in avGFP. This yielded 298 dereplicated sequences from 53 teams across five countries. Student designs explored sequence space distinct from existing avGFP variants in FPbase24 (Figure 3e), using strategies ranging from structure‑guided methods to multi‑stage screening (Extended Data Fig. 6a) and diverse machine learning frameworks including transformer‑based architectures such as ESM25 and Saprot26 (Extended Data Fig. 6b). All submitted designs, computational strategies, and trained models were made openly accessible to promote community learning.
Before synthesis, every sequence underwent biosecurity screening using the open‑source IBBIS Common Mechanism27 (Figure 3d), ensuring no dual‑use concerns while enabling open collaboration. LabscriptAI then adapted the CFPS protocols for multi‑plate processing, optimizing master mix volumes and scheduling sequential measurements (Supplementary Fig. 2). The system characterized all variants under standardized conditions, normalizing fluorescence against batch‑specific fluorescein calibration curves. Among the 298 variants, 53 showed improved fluorescence relative to avGFP in CFPS (Supplementary Table 2). Single and double mutants gave only modest gains, but the proportion of improved variants increased substantially at three or more mutations (Figure 3f, Supplementary Table 2), suggesting that multiple coordinated mutations are required for substantial enhancement, necessitating algorithm‑assisted exploration of large sequence spaces. The top variant, Var1, carries five mutations (Supplementary Table 1) and exhibited an 8.11‑fold improvement over avGFP in CFPS reactions (Figure 3f). It was designed by multi‑model ensemble predictions from six independent models with hierarchical filtering (Extended Data Fig. 6c). This result provides a biologically meaningful demonstration that community‑designed sequences, generated entirely without robot programming by the students, can yield fluorescent proteins competitive with extensively optimized benchmarks.
Benchmark community-designed GFP variants with public database
To rigorously evaluate the CAPE‑winning variants, we compared them against benchmarks in FPbase, a community‑curated database of fluorescent protein photophysical properties. Because CFPS measurements conflate expression level with molecular brightness28, protein purification is required for accurate assessment. LabscriptAI was therefore challenged with the natural language request: “Quantitatively compare CAPE GFP variants with FPbase bright GFP variants in purified form”. The collaboration agent was extended to interface with FPbase via API (Extended Data Fig. 4), retrieving database entries and identifying superfolder GFP (sfGFP, Supplementary Table 1) as a high‑performance benchmark derived from avGFP29. The agent extracted metric specifications from the FPbase glossary and relevant literature30, then transmitted them to LabscriptAI for protocol development.
LabscriptAI first generated updated CFPS scripts incorporating magnetic bead purification with stepwise imidazole washes (Supplementary Fig. 2), a Bradford assay for protein concentration normalization (Supplementary Fig. 3), and spectral collection for photophysical characterization. However, SDS‑PAGE analysis revealed that bead‑based purification from microtiter cultures yielded insufficient purity (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Consequently, we manually performed conventional two‑step column chromatography on selected variants from flask cultures (Extended Data Fig. 7b), a reminder that some steps still require human judgment, though LabscriptAI successfully generated the scripts for the initial high‑throughput pipeline.
The workflow concluded with automated data deposition back to FPbase through the collaboration agent (Extended Data Fig. 4). The system formatted the characterization data according to FPbase standards, including excitation and emission spectra (Extended Data Fig. 7b), quantum yields, brightness, and extinction coefficients (Figure 3g). Measured values for avGFP and sfGFP differed numerically from those reported in FPbase, reflecting different assay conditions. Under our conditions, Var1 exhibited a slightly higher quantum yield (0.80 versus 0.65) and molecular brightness (49.50 versus 41.33) than sfGFP. This indicates that a community‑designed variant, generated without any robot programming by the student teams, can match or exceed the performance of an extensively optimized benchmark, demonstrating the power of accessible, community‑driven protein engineering. 
Enzyme variant screening involving hazardous reagents
To extend LabscriptAI beyond fluorescent proteins, we evaluated enzyme engineering workflows involving hazardous reagents, a domain where chemical automation tools, designed for purified small molecules, offer little guidance. We targeted one‑carbon biomanufacturing using a glycolaldehyde synthase (GALS, Supplementary Table 1) from Pseudomonas putida, which catalyzes formaldehyde condensation into glycolaldehyde and dihydroxyacetone31 (Figure 4a). Formaldehyde is carcinogenic and protein‑denaturing, making robotic automation essential for both safety and throughput.
LabscriptAI was challenged with two advances that exploit its native design for synthetic biology. First, distributing the workflow across a biofoundry (DNA and strain construction, Extended Data Fig. 8) and a fume hood‑enclosed Opentrons OT‑2 (reaction and analysis, Figure 4b), connected by human‑mediated transfers. Second, performing combinatorial mutagenesis at residues V281 and F282 (Figure 4a), where single mutations had previously failed to yield improvements32. The natural language request was: “Create combinatorial double‑site mutagenesis library at V281 and F282, screen for enhanced formaldehyde biotransformation activity using distributed automation.”
LabscriptAI orchestrated the multi‑platform workflow (Figure 4b). In the biofoundry, it generated scripts for automated library construction using a Tecan Fluent with primers encoding 19 amino acid substitutions per position (producing 38 single and 361 double mutants), followed by clone picking, sequence verification, and automated cell cultivation, induction, and washing. The induced cells were then transferred to the fume hood‑enclosed OT‑2, where LabscriptAI scripts automated the preparation of formaldehyde‑containing assay buffer (1.5 g L⁻¹ formaldehyde in 50 mM PBS with cofactors), static biotransformation at room temperature for 24 hours, and supernatant collection with separate derivatization for product quantification. Critically, LabscriptAI generated not only robot scripts but also explicit human safety instructions, specifying personal protective equipment, sealed plate handling procedures, and designated transfer routes (Supplementary Figs. 4‑7). All formaldehyde handling and product detection were confined to the fume hood‑enclosed system, demonstrating how strategic workflow partitioning enhances laboratory safety while maintaining throughput.
Automated screening of whole‑cell biotransformation (50 mM formaldehyde, 24 hours) identified several double mutants with improvements unattainable by single mutations (Figure 4c), validating the combinatorial strategy. Top variants were manually purified and evaluated for in vitro formaldehyde conversion (66.7 mM substrate, 1 mg mL⁻¹ enzyme, 2 hours). Parent GALS yielded 5.3% conversion (1.52 mM glycolaldehyde, 0.17 mM dihydroxyacetone), while the W/L and W/T variants achieved 31.5 % (8.39 mM, 1.41 mM) and 14.9 % (3.30 mM, 1.11 mM) conversion, respectively (Figure 4d).
LabscriptAI further developed automated workflows for enzyme kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 7). The W/L variant showed 3.2‑fold and 7.0‑fold enhancements in catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) for glycolaldehyde and dihydroxyacetone, respectively, driven primarily by increased turnover (5.3‑fold and 6.1‑fold kcat) (Extended Data Fig. 9). The W/T variant improved 2.2‑fold and 3.1‑fold in efficiency, mainly through enhanced substrate binding (0.64‑fold and 0.60‑fold Km) with moderate turnover increases (1.4‑fold and 1.8‑fold). Notably, the W/L variant showed little inhibition even at 1,500 mM formaldehyde (45 g l⁻¹), whereas parent GALS and W/T lost productivity above 500 mM (15 g l⁻¹) (Figure 4e). Given formaldehyde’s well‑characterized protein‑denaturing properties, this substrate tolerance represents a critical advance for industrial biocatalysis.
Finally, the collaboration agent processed enzyme data and metadata to meet heterogeneous repository requirements (Extended Data Fig. 4), from programmatic API submissions (SABIO‑RK33) to manual web forms (STRENDA34), and from XML‑based SBML (EnzymeML35) to spreadsheet templates (BioCatNet36). The framework adapted experimental data to each database’s metadata schemas and controlled vocabularies, eliminating manual reformatting and ensuring cross‑laboratory standardization. This case thus demonstrates that LabscriptAI can handle living systems with hazardous substrates, a class of experiments that chemical automation platforms cannot address, while automating data stewardship for the broader community.
Robotic preparation and quality assurance for the 2025 iGEM distribution kit
To test whether LabscriptAI can support synthetic biology at community scale, we applied it to prepare the 2025 iGEM Distribution Kit, a collection of standardized genetic parts (promoters, reporters, plasmid backbones, composite devices) shipped to hundreds of student teams worldwide for distributed experimentation. Because the kit involves global dissemination of recombinant DNA, responsible manufacturing requires both rigorous quality control and biosecurity oversight.
Starting from dried plasmid DNA samples provided by collection maintainers, the workflow began with bacterial transformation, clonal isolation, cell cultivation, and plasmid DNA isolation on a Tecan Fluent station using established biofoundry workflows (Figure 5a,b; Extended Data Fig. 8). LabscriptAI generated the scripts for downstream steps: plasmid DNA quantification, concentration normalization, and kit plate assembly (Figure 5b). Biosecurity followed a layered framework (Figure 5c): at the curation stage, iGEM applied the Common Mechanism to pre‑screen all reference sequences for dual‑use concerns; after robotic preparation, sequencing deviations from these pre‑cleared references triggered additional biosecurity review.
The 2025 Distribution Kit encompassed 584 genetic parts sourced from multiple groups (iGEM Engineering Committee, FreeGenes, Open Bioeconomy Lab, Asimov). Eleven parts failed to yield transformants despite repeated trials and were not pursued. Whole‑plasmid sequencing was performed on all 573 successfully transformed clones (Figure 5d). Among these, 171 produced perfect matches to the expected reference sequences. Of the non‑matching clones, mutation analysis identified 346 with sequence deviations: 320 harbored a recurrent G‑to‑A substitution in the origin of replication present exclusively in plasmids carrying ampicillin‑resistance genes, and 26 carried other mutations. These clones were included in the distribution with the mismatches annotated. The remaining 56 clones were aligned against the full sequence collection of the kit; 42 produced no hits and were excluded, while 14 had been assigned to incorrect plate positions during upstream processing and were included after correction.
At the biosecurity level, the 346 clones with identifiable mutations were assessed for altered risk profiles, because cloning errors or spontaneous mutagenesis could introduce unanticipated biological functions. The 42 clones that produced no alignment hits to any reference most likely arose from sample contamination or degradation in this instance, yet the same framework would serve to detect erroneous or unauthorized sequence introductions in any automated workflow.
In total, 531 parts passed biosecurity screening and were assembled into the final kit. For assembly (Figure 5e), LabscriptAI integrated plasmid concentration measurements, whole‑plasmid sequencing results, and plate inventory records together with Tecan Fluent hardware configurations as inputs. It then generated executable scripts to dilute plasmid DNA with 0.1 % Cresol Red in TE buffer to a normalized concentration of 2 ng μl⁻¹ in 96‑deep‑well master plates. From these master plates, 3 μl aliquots were dispensed into two 384‑well destination plates, air‑dried, and foil‑sealed to produce 500 distribution kits for shipment to registered iGEM teams worldwide. The system produced a comprehensive sample report detailing final plate positions, sequencing outcomes, and annotated mutations for each distributed part.
This case demonstrates that LabscriptAI can automate regulatory‑grade, community‑scale parts distribution, a uniquely synthetic biology activity with no analog in chemical automation, while embedding biosecurity and quality assurance as non‑bypassable features.
DISCUSSION
Laboratory automation is essential for exploring the large sequence spaces and systematically optimizing biological function that define modern synthetic biology and protein engineering1-4. Liquid‑handling stations, biofoundries, and distributed robotic platforms can scale experiments far beyond manual capacity, yet this potential remains largely unrealized because translating complex experimental designs into robust code demands programming expertise that most biologists lack. Existing approaches have only partially addressed this gap. LLM‑based tools can generate scripts for specific platforms and tasks9-12, but they struggle with multi‑step protocols, hardware heterogeneity, and reproducible debugging. Other autonomous protein engineering platforms37-40 focus on iterative sequence optimization guided by active learning, yet are typically tied to pre‑defined protocols, fixed hardware, and limited safety or data‑integration support. Moreover, almost all of these systems were designed for chemistry, defined small molecules, static reactions, and minimal biosecurity concerns, and fail when applied to synthetic biology, which introduces unique demands: sequence‑level biosecurity, the context‑dependent behavior of living systems, integration with community standards, and support for distributed collaboration across laboratories.
We introduced LabscriptAI to address these demands natively. Across a 90‑task benchmark stratified by difficulty, LabscriptAI passed 96.7 % of tasks, far outperforming direct LLMs (47.8–82.2 %) and a commercial alternative (76.7 %). Experimental validation progressed through increasing biological and operational complexity: cross‑platform iGEM fluorescence calibration, characterization of 298 community‑designed GFP variants (the top five‑mutation variant matched the brightness of superfolder GFP, which carries 11 mutations from extensive directed evolution29), a 7‑fold enhancement in catalytic efficiency and substrate tolerance (from 500 mM to 1,500 mM) for a formaldehyde‑utilizing enzyme, and finally the automated, quality‑assured preparation of 531 biosecurity‑screened genetic parts into 500 iGEM distribution kits shipped worldwide. Notably, integration with the CAPE competition and iGEM exemplifies how LabscriptAI democratizes synthetic biology: students contributed diverse computational models and explored expansive sequence spaces without writing a single line of robot code.
Beyond empirical performance, LabscriptAI illustrates three design principles for LLM-driven automation that are particularly important for synthetic biology. Hierarchical task decomposition separates high-level experimental logic from platform-specific implementation — beginning with human-in-the-loop SOP refinement before any code is produced — allowing biological procedures to be validated and refined at the planning stage. The Precise Refactoring Engine demonstrates that targeted SEARCH/REPLACE modifications are more effective than wholesale regeneration, preserving validated code segments and making debugging interpretable. Knowledge retrieval from curated resources supplies the domain expertise that generic LLMs lack, while separating long-term knowledge from short-term project data enables episodic memory reuse without compromising data integrity. Complementing these architectural features, a multi-layered safety framework establishes a template for responsible use. Biosecurity screening of all community-submitted sequences before synthesis (including a student-accessible Google Colab implementation, Supplementary Fig. 8) embeds safety at the design stage. For community-scale parts distribution, a two-layer framework applies pre-curation sequence screening followed by post-production review triggered by sequencing deviations. Workflow partitioning confines hazardous operations (e.g., formaldehyde handling) to controlled environments, paired with automated execution, explicit safety instructions for manual interventions, and human-in-the-loop oversight at critical decision points. Progressive validation from simulation through physical execution further reduces the likelihood that unsafe or erroneous protocols reach the bench. Together, these principles show how LLM-based automation can be engineered to strengthen, rather than erode, existing biosafety and biosecurity norms.
Bidirectional integration with community databases advances reproducible synthetic biology research in line with FAIR principles41-44 (Extended Data Fig. 4). By retrieving protocols from open repositories (protocols.io), adapting them to specific hardware, and depositing validated implementations, LabscriptAI contributes to a self‑reinforcing cycle of shared methods and data. Automated reconciliation of heterogeneous database formats, via API calls, file format conversions, and structured data exports – reduces barriers to multi‑database deposition. More broadly, dynamic retrieval‑augmented generation from curated community resources enables accurate translation of biological requirements into robotic instructions, helping to establish collaborative ecosystems in which each successful protocol enhances future experimental and computational capabilities.
Several limitations point to development priorities. The current implementation focuses on individual liquid‑handling robots; integration with additional processing and analytical devices would broaden workflow coverage. The framework supports protocol revision in response to intermediate experimental results, but result‑driven adaptation was not evaluated in the case studies reported here. Systematic evaluation of this capability, together with integration with electronic laboratory notebooks, remains future work.
Looking forward, the convergence of more capable language models, broader hardware integration, and expanding community knowledge bases suggests that natural language‑guided automation will increasingly become standard practice in biological laboratories. We envisage extending LabscriptAI from liquid handling to full biofoundry orchestration, coupling it with adaptive experimentation engines that update protocols in response to interim measurements, and embedding it more deeply within FAIR‑compliant data infrastructures. In parallel, the open‑source release of LabscriptAI with built‑in biosafety and biosecurity safeguards will enable community‑driven extension of its capabilities while maintaining responsible use, helping to democratize protein engineering and synthetic biology for the benefit of society.
In conclusion, LabscriptAI serves as both a practical tool for current laboratories and a conceptual blueprint for future LLM‑driven automation. By coupling natural language interfaces with cross‑platform code generation, multi‑layered safety, and deep integration into community data ecosystems, it shows that automation can be made more accessible without compromising rigor or responsibility. As the underlying models, instruments, and datasets continue to evolve, we anticipate that similar frameworks will help transform laboratory practice from bespoke scripting to reproducible, shareable, and collaboratively optimized automated workflows.

Online Methods
Software Framework
LabscriptAI was implemented in Python as a single execution-aware agent harness. To maintain direct control over execution without relying on external orchestration frameworks, the system uses a fixed tool registry guarded by a deterministic action gate, and instantiates a reasoning and acting (ReAct) framework as two control loops: an authoring loop (LabscriptAgentLoop) and a runtime loop (RecoveryOrchestrator). These loops share platform-specific simulators, a deterministic retry buffer for idempotent retries, and disk-persisted episodic case memory. Loop mode controls which tools are visible and which gate policy applies. The web interface (Extended Data Fig. 1, Supplementary Note 1) is provided as an experience demonstration that lets researchers at other laboratories turn an experimental protocol into device-specific code; it was built in React (v18.3.1) and deployed at https://labscriptai.cn/. All quantitative results, including the 90-task benchmark, were produced with the labscriptai Python package and the scripts/ runner, not the web interface. In authoring mode the loop follows a turn-based ReAct cycle: human-in-the-loop SOP validation precedes code generation; each turn may read, write, or patch package files and invoke a simulator. Validation failures are returned to the model as observations and resolved by the Precise Refactoring Engine (Extended Data Fig. 2) via SEARCH/REPLACE edits, with a maximum of nine iterations. Planning, coding, and review are phases of this single loop rather than separate agents.
Platform-specific validation used the simulator distributed with Opentrons software 8.5.1 for Opentrons systems, the PyHamilton module in PyLabRobot 0.1.6 for Hamilton Vantage, and pyFluent, developed in this study, for Tecan Fluent (Extended Data Fig. 5).
A collaboration agent interfaced with external databases and repositories (Extended Data Fig. 4). For protocols.io, the agent inter-converted between natural language SOPs and API-compatible JSON structures for versioned storage and retrieval. For protein databases, custom Python interfaces were implemented to accommodate heterogeneous access methods, formatting requirements, metadata schemas, and submission protocols, enabling automated deposition in compliance with platform-specific requirements and FAIR data principles.
LLM Selection and Prompt Engineering
Production deployments of LabscriptAI used DeepSeek v4-flash via the official API. For the 90-task benchmark and ablation studies (Table 1), LabscriptAI likewise used DeepSeek v4-flash; baseline systems were queried through each system’s native endpoint or OpenRouter as required. Codex used GPT-5.5, and Claude Code used Claude Opus 4.8. Prompts comprised three hierarchical components: system-level instructions, hardware constraint registries specifying valid labware and instruments, and user experimental descriptions. To mitigate hallucinations and ensure API compliance, we implemented a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline that dynamically retrieved context from a curated repository of code examples, utilizing Qwen3-Embedding-8B for vector embeddings and Chroma for vector storage, thereby grounding generation in validated domain knowledge.
Hardware Configuration
For liquid handling robots, the Opentrons OT-2 model was equipped with 1-channel and 8-channel pipettes, along with a Temperature Module and a Magnetic Module. The OT-Flex model was configured with 1-channel and 8-channel pipettes, a Thermocycler Module GEN2, a Heater-Shaker Module, a Magnetic Block, and a Temperature Module, along with the integrated Flex Gripper for labware movement (Figure 3a). The Hamilton Vantage was configured with an 8-channel pipetting arm, a Heater-Shaker Module for incubation and agitation, an integrated Gripper for labware movement, and dedicated carriers for pipette tip racks and reagent reservoirs. The biofoundry work cell configuration (Extended Data Fig. 8) was described previously32, with a Tecan Fluent station with an 8-channel pipette and a 96/384-channel pipette, a Heater-Shaker Module for plate incubation and agitation, a Temperature Module for microcentrifuge tube incubation, and an integrated Gripper for labware movement.
Benchmark Design and Evaluation
A benchmark for evaluating LLM-based liquid-handling script generation was developed by expanding the task set from Inagaki et al.9 from 27 to 90 tasks, with each task annotated for error categorization and ablation analysis (Supplementary Dataset 1). Tasks were stratified across four difficulty tiers: Easy (n=15, basic liquid handling), Medium (n=39, multi-step workflows), Hard (n=29, complex error-prone operations), and Expert (n=7, high-complexity multi-step workflows), with a mean difficulty index of 2.31 on a 1–4 scale. Prompts had a mean length of ~99 words. The benchmark included safety-sensitive scenarios (n=27, ~30%), multi-module orchestration tasks (n=9, ~10%), and parameterized templates (n=15, ~17%); category assignments are listed in Supplementary Dataset 1. Performance was evaluated by simulation pass rate using platform-specific simulators. Ablation studies removed individual architectural components to quantify their contributions.
Strains and Cultivation Conditions
E. coli DH5α (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) was used for plasmid construction and amplification. E. coli BL21(DE3) (Sangon Biotech) served as the recombinant host for protein expression. E. coli BL21(DE3)-Δ6 (ΔfrmA, ΔdhaK, ΔfsaA, ΔfsaB, ΔgldA, ΔglpK)1 was employed for GALS expression and whole-cell FALD biotransformation. E. coli cells were cultured at 37 °C with 220 rpm shaking in Luria-broth (LB) media supplemented with appropriate antibiotics for plasmid maintenance: 50 μg mL-1 kanamycin (Kan) and/or 50 μg mL-1 spectinomycin (Spec). Solid media were prepared by adding 1.5% (w/v) agar. All chemicals were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) or Thermo Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA) unless otherwise noted.
DNA and Strain Construction
All strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 3, 4. Primers and genes (Supplementary Dataset 2) were synthesized by GenScript Biotech (Nanjing, China) or RootPath (Guangzhou, China). Enzymes for recombinant DNA cloning, including Q5 PCR polymerase, restriction endonucleases, and T4 ligase, were purchased from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA, USA). Cell-free GFP expression
CFPS reactions were assembled using the NEBExpress Cell-free E. coli Protein Synthesis System (New England Biolabs), comprising 6 μL of NEBExpress S30 Synthesis Extract, 12.5 μL of 2× Protein Synthesis Buffer, 0.5 μL of T7 RNA polymerase, 0.5 μL of Murine RNase Inhibitor, and 100 ng of template DNA per reaction. Reactions were brought to a final volume of 25 μL with nuclease-free water in 96-well plates and incubated at 37°C for 3h. Fluorescence measurements were performed using an Agilent Microplate Reader with excitation at 480 nm and emission at 520 nm.
Biosecurity Screening and Landscape Visualization of GFP Sequences
Biosecurity screening of student-submitted sequences was performed using a local implementation of the Common Mechanism for DNA Synthesis Screening as previously described27 (https://github.com/ibbis-bio/common-mechanism). The pipeline comprised three steps27: (1) HMM-based matching against curated pathogen sequence profiles to screen sequences homologous to known hazardous factors; (2) two-level taxonomic screening for regulated organisms via protein-level BLASTX/DIAMOND (NCBI nr) and nucleotide-level BLASTN (NCBI core_nt); (3) low-risk functional annotation of pathogen-matched sequences using benign genes like housekeeping genes to filter false positives and identify genuine dual-use biosecurity risks. To promote responsible research awareness among CAPE participants, a cloud-based implementation was provided as a Google Colab notebook for self-screening prior to design submission (available at colab.research.google.com/drive/1JvIxU67nSD5IMSvRqvCYnGl8ERaVLHCT).
To visualize the sequence landscape, CAPE submissions (n = 298) and FPbase references (n = 140) were encoded with the esm2_t12_35M_UR50D25 model and dimensionality was reduced via t-SNE45 (perplexity=30, PCA initialization). The resulting 2D projection incorporated kernel density estimation (KDE) to display distribution densities, with the top 10 performing CAPE variants, wild-type avGFP, and benchmark sfGFP sequences overlaid as distinct data points.
Magnetic bead-based protein purification in microtiter plates
His-tagged proteins from CFPS reactions were purified using Ni-NTA Magnetic Beads (Smart-Lifesciences, Changzhou, China). Bead slurry was resuspended in five bead volumes of Binding Buffer by vortexing and transferred to 96-well plates as 100 μL aliquots per well. Beads were equilibrated by magnetic separation on the magnetic block, supernatant removal, and two washes with 100 μL of Lysis Buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, pH 8.0), with resuspension by pipetting 10 times between washes. CFPS reactions (50 μL) were added to equilibrated beads and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature with 1,000 rpm shaking on the heater-shaker module. Beads were magnetically separated, and the supernatant was removed. Non-specifically bound proteins were removed through two sequential washes with 150 μL of Wash Buffer containing 0 mM or 20 mM imidazole, respectively. Target proteins were eluted by resuspending beads in 150 μL of Elution Buffer containing 250 mM imidazole and incubating for 10 min.
Bradford assay for protein quantification
Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). A standard curve was generated using bovine serum albumin (BSA) diluted in PBS to concentrations ranging from 0 to 1.5 mg mL-1. For the assay, 5 µL of each standard or sample was mixed with 250 µL of G250 dye reagent in a 96-well plate. Following a 10-minute incubation at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader, and protein concentrations were calculated by comparison to the BSA standard curve.
Manual GFP expression and purification
Transformed BL21(DE3) cells were cultured in 1 L of LB medium at 37 °C until OD600 reached 0.6-0.8, followed by induction with 0.5 mM IPTG at 18°C for 16-20 hours. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in Lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Cell lysis was performed by sonication on ice, cleared lysate obtained by centrifugation (4,000 × g, 30 min, 4°C) was applied to Ni-NTA agarose (Qiagen), and proteins were eluted with 250 mM imidazole. Following concentration using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filters (30 kDa MWCO, Millipore), proteins were further purified by size exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 pg 16/600 column using an ÄKTA prime PLUS system (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with 25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0, with elution monitored at 280 nm absorbance (A280).

Construction of GALS Mutants
GALS variant libraries were created using a previously reported biofoundry workflow32. First, site-directed mutations were introduced via PCR using designed primers. Following two-fragment Gibson assembly, 5 μL of reaction mix was used to transform 50 μL of competent E. coli DH5α in 96-well format (4°C for 30 min, 42°C for 60 s), which were then recovered in 200 μL of LB at 37 °C and 800 rpm for 2 h before plating on selective agar medium. Single colonies were isolated using a QPix 420 colony picker and cultured overnight in 500 μL selective LB at 37 °C and 800 rpm. For each construct, three random colonies were picked for sequence verification. A representative collection of mutant strains covering all 19 single mutants and 361 double mutants were then selected by hit picking. Plasmid DNA was extracted on a KingFisher Flex module using the 96 Mag-MK Plasmid DNA Mini-Preps Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). To create strain libraries, plasmid DNA was then used to transform BL21(DE3)-Δ6, and two independent clones were selected for subsequent phenotypic screening.
Whole-cell FALD Biotransformation for Mutant Screening
Mutant colonies were picked into 96 deep-well microplates (Jetbiofil, Guangzhou, China) containing 200 uL of LB+Kan and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Cultures were diluted 1:100 into 1 mL of fresh LB+Kan containing 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h followed by 20 h at 30°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3,300 × g for 5 min, washed twice using phosphate-buffered saline (50 mM PBS, pH 7.4), and resuspended in assay buffer (50 mM PBS, 5 mM MgSO4, 0.5 mM thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), 1.5 g L-1 FALD, pH 7.4). After 30°C incubation for 24 h, supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 3,300 × g for 5 min for product analysis.
Spectrometric Assays for GA and DHA Detection
For spectrometric GALD detection, diphenylamine (DPA) reagent was prepared by dissolving 1.5 g diphenylamine in 100 mL of glacial acetic acid followed by addition of 1.5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid. Sample and standard solutions (30 μL) were mixed with 150 μL of DPA reagent in 96-well PCR plates (Jetkeen, Dongguan, China), incubated at 90°C for 30 min, and endpoint absorbance was measured at 650 nm following transfer to 96-well microtiter plates (Corning 3599).
For spectrometric DHA detection, 90 μL of sample or standard solutions were dried at room temperature in 96-well microtiter plates in a chemical fume hood. Samples were reconstituted in 90 μL of 50 mM PBS (pH 7.4), followed by addition of 60 μL Buffer A (0.2 mg mL-1 galactose oxidase, 24 U mL-1 horse radish peroxidase, 50 mM PBS, 5 mM MgSO4, pH 7.4) and then 50 μL Buffer B (3.2 mM ABTS, pH 7.4). Absorbance at 410 nm was monitored for 20 min.
Manual GALS Protein Expression and Purification
Genes encoding the parent and mutant GALS were assembled into pET28m expression vector via Gibson Assembly. E. coli BL21(DE3) cells carrying recombinant plasmids were cultivated in 5 mL of LB+Kan at 37 °C for 16-20 hrs and subsequently scaled to 1 L cultures. When OD600 reached 0.6~0.8, IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM, followed by 18 °C induction for 20 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,000 × g and resuspended in 50 mL of Lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM MgSO4, pH 7.5). 
Cell lysis was performed using a high-pressure homogenizer (JNBIO, Guangzhou, China), and debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The clarified lysate was loaded onto a nickel affinity column (GE Healthcare), washed with 50 mL of wash buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl buffer, 5 mM MgSO4, and 20 mM imidazole, pH 7.5) and eluted with 20 mL of elution buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM MgSO4, and 250 mM imidazole, pH 7.5). Purified protein was concentrated and buffer-exchanged into 50 mM PBS with 5 mM MgSO4 (pH 7.4) using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter device (Millipore, USA, 30 kDa MWCO). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime).
In vitro Enzyme Kinetics Characterization
Initial reaction rates were measured at 37 °C in 50 mM PBS (pH 7.4) containing 5 mM MgSO4 and 0.5 mM TPP upon addition of 1 mg mL-1 purified enzyme. FALD substrate concentrations ranged from 2 to 1,500 mM. Kinetic parameters kcat and Km were estimated by measuring initial velocities and curve-ﬁtting to the Michaelis-Menten equation or allosteric sigmoidal equation, as appropriate, using GraphPad Prism 9 software. All measurements were performed in triplicate.
Automated Production and Quality Assurance of the iGEM Distribution Kit
Dried plasmid DNA samples were recovered by E. coli transformation, colony isolation, overnight cultivation, and plasmid extraction using the previously reported biofoundry workflow described above for GALS library construction. Frozen strain stocks were prepared by mixing 100 μL of cell cultures with 100 μL of 40% glycerol for storage at -80°C. Plasmid concentration was measured by Stunner (Unchained Labs, Pleasanton, CA, USA), normalized to 2 ng μL-1 in 0.1% Cresol Red in TE buffer in 96-deep-well master plates, and dispensed as 3 μL aliquots into 384-well destination plates, which were air-dried and foil-sealed before shipment. For the whole-plasmid sequencing data, a custom Biopython pipeline was developed to perform global alignment against reference sequences, mismatch enumeration, and structural variant detection. Ambiguous alignments were manually inspected in SnapGene (v6.0.2). All mutation-containing and no-hit sequences were subsequently screened using the Google Colab implementation of the Common Mechanism (V1.0.3) with taxonomy modules disabled (--skip-taxonomy, --skip-nt).
